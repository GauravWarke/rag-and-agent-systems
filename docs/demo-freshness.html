The monitor's assumptions, stated plainly: $38/month running cost, change batches processed every 6 hours, 1% of a full rebuild in fixed overhead per batch, partial re-embedding at 1.35× the proportional cost, and 88% detection recall — the 12% of changes it misses stay stale until a fallback full rebuild 30 days later. A scheduled full rebuild has 100% recall by construction: it re-embeds everything, whether or not it noticed the change.

Reading the results

Each document that has changed since the last rebuild is stale: answers built from it may be wrong. How often you rebuild is your cadence. A slower cadence means more stale documents at any moment.

Not all stale documents matter equally. The table weights each type by how often people ask about it, not by how many pages it has. That is query-weighted staleness, and it is why a tiny pricing section can matter more than a huge policy library.

In short: rebuilding on a schedule is often cheaper than watching for changes. Watching wins when a full rebuild is expensive, or when only a small part of your documents changes often. How well the watcher notices changes, its recall, decides the rest.

Go deeper: the full analysis

Start with the gap between of corpus and of queries. On the product profile, pricing pages are 4% of the index and 14% of the traffic, and they change roughly eighteen times faster than the policy library. A freshness metric averaged over the corpus would call that index 96% clean and be worthless. Staleness only means something weighted by what people actually ask.

The monitor is frequently not the cheapest option, and that's the honest finding. At the default settings a nightly cron job beats it. Detection only starts paying when a full rebuild is genuinely expensive — drag cost per full rebuild up past roughly $220 and the ordering flips, because at that point re-embedding everything 30 times a month costs more than the machinery required to avoid it. Below that line, building a freshness monitor is over-engineering, and the correct answer is a scheduled job and a good night's sleep.

Recall is the detector's real specification, not latency. Six-hour detection sounds like the headline number, and it isn't — set recall to 100% and the monitor would win every configuration on this page outright. It loses where it loses because 12% of changes slip past content hashing: an upstream source that was edited without changing bytes, a document that is still accurate-looking but now wrong because the world moved. Those sit stale for a month. When someone sells you a change-detection system, the number to ask about is what fraction of real changes it catches, not how fast it reacts to the ones it does.

The other regime worth finding is the low end of cost of one stale answer. Below about $10 the monitor wins again, for an unglamorous reason: when stale answers are cheap, accuracy stops mattering and the strategy that spends least on indexing simply wins. Two different regimes, same winner, completely different reasoning — which is why the table shows every strategy rather than just the winner.

Worth noticing: switch to the policy profile, where almost nothing changes, and the monitor wins comfortably. That's the opposite of the intuition that slow corpora don't need freshness tooling — and the reason is that only 11% of that corpus moves in a month, so re-embedding just the changed part is very cheap. Nearly half the staleness comes from a single class — announcements is 6% of the index and changes about seven times faster than anything else in that corpus. A slow corpus with one fast class buried inside it is the best case for detection, not the worst.
